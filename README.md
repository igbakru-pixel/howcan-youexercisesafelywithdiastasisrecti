<h1><strong>How Can You Exercise Safely With Diastasis Recti?</strong></h1>
<p><em><span style="font-weight: 400;">This article is for general information only. It is not medical advice. Check with your doctor or a pelvic floor physical therapist before starting any exercise program, especially after childbirth.</span></em></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The best exercise program for diastasis recti is one that trains the deep core muscles with slow, controlled movement and steady breathing, then builds up gradually while keeping your belly flat.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It usually combines breathing work, pelvic floor exercises, gentle transverse abdominis (deep core) training and a careful return to bigger moves.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">No single branded routine has been proven better than the rest, so the smartest pick is a progressive plan that fits your stage of recovery and, ideally, comes with a physical therapist's guidance.</span></p>
<h2><strong>The Short Answer: Start Deep, Go Slow, Watch for Doming</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Cleveland Clinic says the best exercises for diastasis recti are the ones that engage the deep abdominals, and that most involve deep breathing and slow, controlled movements. Many common ab exercises, such as crunches, can make things worse.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The warning sign to watch is doming or coning, where your belly pushes out in a ridge down the middle during effort.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If you see it, that move is too hard for now.</span></p>
<h2><strong>What Diastasis Recti Is</strong></h2>
<p><a href="https://honest-frog-12znf5m.mystrikingly.com/"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Diastasis recti</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> is a separation of the two sides of the rectus abdominis, the "six-pack" muscles, along the midline.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A 2021 systematic review reports it affects about 60 percent of women six weeks after birth and 32.5 percent at 12 months.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Common signs include a bulge or doming along the middle of the belly, low back pain, poor posture and trouble with everyday lifting.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A doctor or physical therapist can measure the gap with fingers, calipers or ultrasound, and Healthline advises having any gap wider than two finger lengths evaluated.</span></p>

<h2><strong>What the Research Says About Exercise</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Exercise is the standard first treatment, but the evidence is less tidy than most programs suggest.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A 2023 systematic review looked at 16 trials with 698 postnatal women.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It found moderate-certainty evidence that abdominal exercise led to a small reduction in the gap between the muscles, about 0.43 cm.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">You can read the abstract of that</span><a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36934466/"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">2023 meta-analysis</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> yourself.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Other reviews agree that questions remain.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A 2021 review of seven trials said there is very low-quality evidence to recommend specific exercise programs, and a 2024 scoping review found no consensus on the most effective routine.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A 2025 meta-analysis of 34 trials concluded that isotonic abdominal exercises, especially combined with electrical stimulation, were the best conservative option.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Taken together, the research supports exercise, but it cannot name a single winning program.</span></p>
<h2><strong>What a Good Program Includes</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Most physical therapists build a plan around a few pieces.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In one survey cited in the 2021 review, 89 percent of women's health physical therapists recommended transversus abdominis exercises and 87 percent recommended pelvic floor exercises.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A solid program usually has:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Breathing:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Slow belly or "360-degree" breathing that lets the ribs and belly expand, with an exhale during effort.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Pelvic floor work:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Gentle lifts and releases that coordinate with your breath.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Deep core activation:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Drawing in the lower belly slightly, without bracing hard.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Gradual progression:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Moving from lying down to hands and knees to standing and loaded work.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>A "no doming" rule:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Stop or simplify any move that makes your belly bulge.</span></li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>Exercises to Avoid at First</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Several medical sources list the same moves to avoid early on, including crunches, sit-ups, front planks, twisting movements, push-ups and heavy lifting without core support.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">WebMD adds that some yoga poses and holding positions on hands and knees can make separation worse for some people.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Harvard Health says movements that put excessive pressure on the abdominal wall can worsen the separation.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Advice varies on planks and bird-dog moves.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">UT Southwestern notes that planks and bird dogs strengthen the transverse abdominis, while Hospital for Special Surgery therapists say you can progress to planks and bicycles later, as long as you can hold the contraction without doming.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The takeaway is timing: these moves are for later, once your core can control pressure.</span></p>
<h2><strong>A Sample Progression</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Think of recovery as phases, and move on only when the current phase feels easy and your belly stays flat.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This is a general outline, not a prescription.</span></p>
<ol>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Phase 1:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Belly breathing, pelvic tilts and gentle pelvic floor lifts, lying on your back with knees bent.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Phase 2:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Deep core activation with heel slides, knee drops and modified bird dogs.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Phase 3:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Standing and functional work such as squats and carries, then slow return to harder core moves if there is no doming.</span></li>
</ol>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Short, frequent sessions beat long, intense ones. Exhale on the effort part of every move.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Do You Need Surgery or a Belly Band?</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Most people do not.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Banner Health says surgery is rarely needed and may be considered for severe cases, and the 2024 scoping review says it is typically reserved for severe cases where conservative treatment fails.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Banner also notes that support devices such as belly bands do not repair the separation and should be used alongside strengthening exercises.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The</span><a href="https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22346-diastasis-recti"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">Cleveland Clinic guide</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> adds that it is never too late to work on diastasis recti, even years after your last baby.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Safety Notes</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Get your doctor's approval before exercising after delivery, and again if you have had a cesarean birth, pelvic pain or leaking.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Stop any exercise that causes sharp pain, doming, pressure in the pelvis or leaking.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Do not rely on an online program in place of medical care if you have significant incontinence, back pain or a visible bulge.</span></p>
<h2><strong>When to See a Doctor or Physical Therapist</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">See a doctor or pelvic floor physical therapist if the gap is wide, your belly is filled with simple tasks, you have back or pelvic pain, or you leak urine.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A physical therapist can measure the gap and tailor exercises.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Seek urgent care right away if you have a painful, firm bulge that will not go back in, especially with nausea or vomiting, because this can signal a hernia that needs treatment.</span></p>
